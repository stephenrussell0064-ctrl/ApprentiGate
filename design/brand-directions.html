Three directions, one decision

Three visual identities were built as token sets with working hero mocks, then judged against the brief before any was developed further. This is what was made, what was chosen, and why the other two were rejected.

ApprentiGate Direction B selected For Senior User sign-off

The constraint that decided it

The standard way to make a business look credible is to show proof: client logos, certifications, case studies, customer counts, star ratings. Every one of those is forbidden here, because ApprentiGate has no customers yet and inventing them is not an option.

That is not an obstacle to design around — it is the design problem. Credibility has to come from typography, structure and plain honesty instead of borrowed proof. So each direction was tested one way: does it still look credible with the proof panels removed? One of the three did not, and that is why it lost.

Why it lost

It reads as a management consultancy — one of the four things the brief says the site must not resemble. Quiet authority suits a firm whose authority is already established. ApprentiGate's is not, and cannot be evidenced anywhere on the site. Wearing the visual language of seniority without the substance behind it reads as a bluff to exactly the sceptical managing director this site is built for.

Its warm ground and metallic accent also sit one step from a house style the brief explicitly warns against.

Why it won

It is the only one of the three that is true. ApprentiGate is a managed service that intends to become a platform, and this is what operations tooling actually looks like. The visual language is not borrowed from a more established kind of company; it describes the thing being built.

The monospace labels do real work. They read as this business handles the administrative detail — which is the actual proposition — without making a single claim that would need a source. That is credibility from texture rather than assertion, which is precisely what a business with no track record needs.

The teal is a deliberate move away from default corporate blue. Apprenticeships and education are saturated with blue and purple; this is distinctive without being loud, and it is dark enough to carry no "eco" or "sustainability" reading.

Its risk, stated plainly: executed lazily, this becomes generic business software. Three things hold it apart — the monospace layer, the relay band below, and strict whitespace discipline. If any is quietly dropped as pages are added, the identity stops being distinctive.

Why it lost

It is the most generic of the three. Deep blue, rounded corners and a warm accent is the house style of a very large number of business websites. For a company whose entire problem is being unknown, looking like everything else is a real cost.

The rounder shapes and warmer accent also drift toward a friendly-education look. Either choice alone is defensible; together, on an education subject, they compound in the wrong direction.

The signature element

Every identity needs one thing it is remembered by. Here it is the relay band — and it was chosen because it is simultaneously the most important information on the site.

A visitor's first assumption will be that ApprentiGate is a training provider or a recruitment agency. Correcting that is the main job the page has. Making the correction into the recurring visual device means the brand and the message become the same thing: every time the motif appears, it reinforces the proposition rather than decorating it.

Employer Employs and manages the apprentice
ApprentiGate Handles the process in between
Training provider Delivers the training

On the gate idea: it is used exactly once, in the app mark, as a slot cut clean through a solid block — a way through rather than a barrier. There is no archway imagery anywhere, and the relay band deliberately shows an open path between three parties rather than a threshold to be admitted past.

What we need you to confirm

The Stage 2 gate needs the Senior User to look at this and answer four questions. Please view it on your own phone as well as a desktop — most recipients of a cold email will open it on a phone.

  • It does not look like a college or a training provider.
  • It does not look like a recruitment agency.
  • It does not look like a government department or a children's education brand.
  • You would take a cold email from this business seriously.
